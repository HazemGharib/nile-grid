# 10 — Storyline: Nile Grid

**Status:** chosen on 2026-11-27. Hazem selected storyline option 1, *Nile Grid*, and asked that **each governorate chapter have its own distinct mission story**, all tied to one Horus Corp arc.

**Chapters are non-linear:** the player may play any shipped governorate in any order. **Ismailia is being designed first but is not necessarily the first level.** Its *internal* mission order (west → east) stays fixed; see [11-chapter-ismailia.md](11-chapter-ismailia.md).

**Win condition:** the story is won only when **every shipped governorate chapter is complete**. The reward is a **pixel-art ending cutscene** ([14-ending-cutscene.md](14-ending-cutscene.md)).

Crew names are **approved**: Nour Hassan (male), Adel Mansour (male), and Laila Farouk (the only girl) ([12-characters.md](12-characters.md)). Gameplay is **action combined with stealth and puzzle-solving**.

## Premise

Egypt, **2077**. A fictional corporation, **Horus Corp**, has seized control of the Nile’s water and the national power grid — together nicknamed the **Nile Grid** — and rules each governorate from a **surveillance tower** there. Ships, pumps, and streetlights all answer to the same closed system. The Eye (Horus Corp’s logo and its camera net) is on every tower.

The players are a small crew of **couriers, hackers, and mechanics**, drawn from the *Nile Neon* concept roster. They free governorates **in any order the player chooses**. Each chapter is one governorate map, with a **different** central mission that could only happen in that place. Completing a chapter weakens Horus Corp’s hold on the Grid. No chapter is a prerequisite for another; optional NPC lines may mention a city the player has already freed.

The game is Egyptian cyberpunk, not a commentary on real governments, companies, or living people. No real political figures, parties, or officials appear.

## Tone

- Street-level and hopeful, not grimdark. Neon sits on real streets; the cities still feel lived-in.
- Respect for real places, history, and faith. Mosques, churches, temples, museums, and war memorials are landmarks and civilian spaces. They are never raid targets, never jokes, and never destroyed for a mission beat.
- Combat and infiltration belong in streets, yards, industrial sites, and corporate floors.
- Heritage is something the crew **protects**. Horus Corp is the one that brands and exploits it.
- Humour can be dry and Egyptian; it should not punch down at a city, a community, or a monument.

## The overarching arc

### Beginning

The crew is not an army. They are people who already move through the Grid’s cracks: a courier who knows the alleys, a mechanic who keeps illegal generators alive, a hacker who paints over corporate cameras. The player may start in any shipped city. Wherever they begin, the first job is local and concrete — reopen *that* place’s water, port, archive, or street.

**Ismailia’s** story, when the player opens it, is this: Horus Corp has taken the Suez Canal’s guidance system and halted ship traffic. The lake town chokes; freight dies on the water. The job is to **get the canal moving again** ([11-chapter-ismailia.md](11-chapter-ismailia.md)). Winning Ismailia does not topple Horus Corp. It returns a stretch of water to the people who work it and proves a tower can fall.

### Middle

Each governorate is a new kind of problem, not a reskin of “climb the tower.” The canal’s two mouths, a coastal archive, a heritage concession, the water at Aswan, and the Eye in Cairo are all self-contained chapters. Allies appear in each place and do not all travel with the crew. As more towers fall, Horus Corp tightens the ones that remain and starts starving cities that have slipped. Optional lines can acknowledge a city already freed; nothing in the design should block a chapter because another is still locked.

Candidate chapters (not a play order) are listed below: Ismailia, Port Said, Suez, Alexandria, Luxor, Aswan, and Cairo. Which of them ship besides Ismailia is still open.

### End

The story ends only when **every shipped chapter is complete**, in whatever order the player finished them. The reward is a short **pixel-art ending cutscene** ([14-ending-cutscene.md](14-ending-cutscene.md)).

The last movement is restoration, not a fantasy of ruling the country. Across the finished maps, water and light return. The Eye goes dark as a system. The Grid is handed back to a **fictional civilian compact** of the cities — engineers, pilots, neighbourhood councils — not to any real institution. The towers go dark as brands and stay in the skyline as emptied frames, or come down, as later art decides. The crew goes back to being people on the street, only now the streetlights belong to the street.

The Aswan chapter is still “take back the water” and the Cairo chapter is still “shut the Eye.” Those are the *stories* of those maps, not a required sequence. The ending cutscene must work even if Cairo or Aswan was finished first, or last, or in the middle.

## Factions

### Horus Corp (antagonist)

A fictional megacorp that branded itself with the Eye. It sells “order, water, and light” and means monopoly. Each governorate tower is a node: cameras, ration gates, and a local warden unit of **corporate security** (the *Nile Neon* enemy). The Corp occupies real-looking civic and industrial buildings in the fiction; this is not a statement about any real authority, including the real Suez Canal Authority. Employees who appear are invented. There are no named living people.

### The Current (allies)

A loose, unofficial network of couriers, mechanics, hackers, canal workers, and neighbours. It is not an army and not a political party. In each city the crew meets local faces of the Current — a pilot, a gardener, a station vendor — who want their water, their port, or their street back. The crew is one cell, not the whole movement.

### Civilians

Shopkeepers, crews on the water, families in the old quarters, museum and park staff. They give the map life, side jobs, and shelter. They are not cannon fodder.

## How chapters link

- **One map, one story.** A chapter is won by finishing *that* city’s mission, not by a generic “destroy the tower” copy.
- **One Grid, no gates.** Towers share a network. A freed node weakens the Eye and can be mentioned later, but it is **not** a key that another chapter requires. The player may open any shipped map from the chapter select (and, if we later add travel beats by rail, river, or canal, those are flavour, not locks).
- **People carry over, lightly.** A pilot or a message from one city may appear in another if that city is already freed. The full roster does not have to travel every time.
- **The world stays.** A freed governorate can be revisited later if the project grows that far; it is not required for the first playable build.
- **The ending waits.** Only when all shipped chapters are done does the pixel-art ending cutscene play.

## Candidate chapters (no play order)

These are **one-paragraph mission ideas**, not built maps, and **not a sequence**. Geography and landmarks for those cities have not been researched yet the way Ismailia has. All of them should follow the same rules: real relative geography when a map is built, no real living people, no damage to sacred or memorial sites. Each chapter must be completable on its own, whether or not Ismailia (or any other map) has been finished.

### Port Said — the Mediterranean gate

Horus Corp has turned Port Said’s harbour into a toll and a trap: a fictional boom and a checkpoint net sit on the canal’s Mediterranean mouth, and the waterfront warehouses hold seized cargo. The mission is a dock story, not a tower repeat — fog, cranes, bonded sheds, and a run along the corniche to seize harbour control, lift the boom, and let ships reach the sea. If Ismailia has already been freed, NPCs can mention the guidance coming back; if it has not, the mouth is still worth opening on its own. The historic arcades and waterfront stay recognisable; the fight stays in yards and corporate annexes, not in civic monuments.

### Suez — the Red Sea gate

The southern door of the canal is a different machine: heat, shipyards, tanks, and the Red Sea terminal. Horus Corp is bleeding the southern canal through a fictional fuel-and-data pipeline and can lock this entrance on its own. The mission is industrial and harsh — cut the pipeline, free the southern lock, and reopen the Red Sea gate. Distinct from Port Said in climate, palette, and problem: less fog and customs, more metal, glare, and a long industrial waterfront. Other canal chapters, if already done, can be acknowledged; they are not required.

### Alexandria — the closed archive

The Corp did not only take water and power; it took memory. In Alexandria a coastal server farm and a “closed library” of Grid records sit under a fictional concession on the corniche — the proof of how the water rights were seized, and a siphon on the city’s light. The mission is wet, windy, and about knowledge: follow tram lines and sea walls, slip a data centre, and copy or free the archive without turning the city’s real cultural buildings into dungeons. What the crew learns can colour later visits to other maps if those chapters are still open; it must not be a required key.

### Luxor — the heritage concession

Horus Corp runs a night-time “heritage concession”: holograms, tourist gates, and power taps laid over the temples and the west bank, draining the towns to feed a show. The mission is to shut the concession grid **without harming the ancient sites**. Combat stays outside the monuments. Inside the archaeological areas the work is stealth, puzzle, and protection — kill the projectors, cut the taps, and leave the stones darker and quieter than the crew found them. This chapter should feel like a night visit, not a raid on Egypt’s past.

### Aswan — the water

Here the story stops being about traffic and becomes about the river itself. A fictional Horus control layer sits on the water-release system at the High Dam, treated as a real engineering landmark, not as a political prop and not as a thing to blow up. The mission is a tense, relatively quiet infiltration: disconnect the Corp from the water and return release control to a fictional civil engineering post. Granite, river light, and long sight-lines; less street neon than the delta cities. This chapter can be played first or last; until it is done, the Corp still has a hand on the river, wherever else the crew has already been.

### Cairo — the Eye

This chapter is the capital as a living mega-city, not a single corridor, and **not a required finale**. Horus Corp’s central brain — the Eye — sits in a **fictional** tower district placed in real geography, never on a real palace, ministry, mosque, or church. The mission is to walk a city of quarters and river crossings, shed the surveillance net, and shut the core. It should feel denser and more social than the smaller maps: more civilians, more alleys, more ways in, and a climax that is a disconnection, not a fantasy of ruling Cairo. If Cairo is finished before other chapters, the Eye can go dark locally; the country-wide ending still waits until every shipped map is done.

## Respect rules (all chapters)

- No real living people, and no real political parties or current officials.
- Do not destroy, loot, or stage fights inside mosques, churches, temples, tombs, museums, or war memorials.
- Real authorities and companies are not characters. Horus Corp and the Current are fictional.
- When a real landmark is used, keep its silhouette and its relative position; layer cyberpunk on top, as already agreed for Ismailia.

## What this file does not decide

- Which candidate chapters besides Ismailia actually ship.
- Combat numbers, timers, and ability cooldown tuning (the genre is decided).
- Final faces and animation; names are approved.
- The game’s public title and the GitHub repository name. *Nile Grid* is the storyline name and a **candidate** title only.
